Fine needle scratched delicate lines into the whale bone's polished surface as Osgood worked another maritime scene into the traditional scrimshaw piece, building the image through hundreds of careful, individual strokes. This particular technique dated back centuries.

His apprentice, Wilhelmina, prepared the ink that would eventually fill the scratched lines, mixing it to the precise consistency that would settle properly into the fine grooves without bleeding beyond their edges. "Ink's ready when you need it," she said.

Osgood finished the ship's rigging detail and stepped back to examine his progress, checking the composition's balance before continuing work on the scene's remaining elements. "The proportions are working well," he said.

Wilhelmina watched him continue scratching delicate detail into the whale's form, absorbing the subtle needle pressure and angle that produced such convincingly varied line weights across the developing image. "How long has this piece taken so far?" she asked.

Osgood considered the question while continuing his careful work, genuinely uncertain of the exact hours invested in this particular commission's intricate maritime scene. "Weeks, honestly," he said. "But scrimshaw was never meant to be rushed."
