Granite dust coated Osborne's gloves as he set another stone step into the eroded switchback, tamping it firm before checking its level against the trail's established grade. National park trail crews maintained hundreds of miles through methods barely changed in decades.

His crew partner, Persimmon, hauled another stone from the collection pile, selecting pieces with the right dimensions for the specific gaps still needing attention along this particularly steep section. "This one might work for the next step," she said.

Osborne examined the stone she'd offered, testing its fit against the gap before deciding whether it needed additional shaping or was ready for immediate placement. "Close," he said. "Little bit of shaping and this'll drop right in perfectly."

Persimmon set the stone aside for Osborne's shaping work and continued searching the pile for the following step's requirements, the physically demanding work settling into its familiar rhythm after years of trail crew experience. "Found another good candidate," she called.

Osborne finished shaping his current stone and set it into position, testing its stability with his full weight before moving to the next step in the switchback's slow, methodical reconstruction. "That's solid," he said. "Six more steps and this section's done."
