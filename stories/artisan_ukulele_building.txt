Koa wood grain caught the workshop light as Malia bent the ukulele's side pieces around a heated form, working the traditional bending iron technique that shaped flat wood into the instrument's distinctive curved body. Custom ukuleles demanded careful wood selection.

Her apprentice, Keoni, prepared the top and back plates, thicknessing them to the precise dimensions that would give this particular instrument its intended tonal characteristics once fully assembled. "Plates are thicknessed and ready," he said.

Malia examined the plates he'd prepared, tapping each one to check the tap tone that experienced luthiers used to judge whether the wood was properly prepared for assembly. "These sound perfect," she said. "Nice bright tap tone."

Keoni watched her continue bending the side pieces, absorbing the subtle heat and pressure control that prevented the beautiful koa wood from scorching or cracking during the demanding bending process. "How do you know when it's ready to bend?" he asked.

Malia continued her careful work, considering the question that combined years of experience with an almost intuitive feel for the wood's moisture and temperature. "You'll feel it," she said. "The wood tells you when it's ready to move."
