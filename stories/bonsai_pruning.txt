Small shears clicked precisely as Michiko studied the juniper's silhouette from several angles, deciding which branches disrupted the tree's carefully cultivated shape before committing to another cut. Twenty years of shaping this particular tree had taught her patience above all else.

Her student, Fitzgerald, watched from a respectful distance, sketchbook open to document the session's decisions for his own developing understanding of bonsai design principles. "How do you know which branch to remove?" he asked. "They all look important to me."

Michiko considered the question while studying the tree's negative space, the gaps between branches that mattered as much as the branches themselves. "I'm not looking at branches," she said. "I'm looking at the shape they're not allowing to emerge yet."

Fitzgerald leaned closer to study the specific branch she then removed, trying to understand the decision through the tree's altered silhouette rather than any explicit rule she could articulate. "I think I almost see it," he said, tilting his head to view the tree from a different angle.

Michiko smiled faintly and made one more small cut, stepping back to assess the juniper's evolving form with the same careful attention she'd given it for two decades. "Almost seeing it is how this starts," she said. "The rest comes with years of looking."
