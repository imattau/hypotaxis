Chisel rang against granite as Ferdinand shaped another stone to fit the dry-stone wall's next course, testing the fit repeatedly against its neighbors before setting it permanently into position. No mortar meant every stone had to find its own perfect balance.

His apprentice, Rosalind, sorted through the pile of quarried stones, searching for pieces with the right dimensions to fill the specific gap Ferdinand had identified in the wall's developing pattern. "This one might work," she said, hefting a promising candidate.

Ferdinand took the stone she'd found and tested it against the gap, rotating it through several orientations before finding the angle that created a stable, interlocking fit. "Perfect," he said. "See how it locks against both neighbors? That's what makes these walls last centuries."

Rosalind watched him tap the stone into its final resting position, absorbing the intuitive knowledge of weight and balance that seemed to come so naturally to him after decades of practice. "How do you just know which stone will fit?" she asked.

Ferdinand smiled and continued scanning the stone pile for the next piece, the question as familiar as the craft itself after years of teaching eager apprentices this ancient skill. "You'll know too," he said. "Give it a few hundred more stones."
