Fine abrasive grit ground steadily beneath Casimir's palms as he worked the glass blank in slow figure-eight strokes, shaping the mirror that would eventually become his telescope's primary optic. Amateur mirror-making took months of patience most modern astronomers skipped entirely.

His workshop neighbor, Wilhelmina, tested the mirror's developing curve with a simple foucault tester, checking the parabola's accuracy against the readings from his last testing session. "Curve's coming along nicely," she said. "Maybe another twenty hours of fine grinding before polishing."

Casimir paused his grinding motion and examined the glass under the workshop light, checking for any visible scratches that might have crept in during this session's work. "No new scratches that I can see," he said. "Good, that would've meant starting this stage over."

Wilhelmina set up the tester again after his brief pause, checking the mirror's surface against the zone readings that would eventually determine if the parabola was accurate enough for serious astronomical use. "Zones are matching well," she said. "You're getting close to a genuinely good mirror here."

Casimir resumed his steady grinding rhythm, motivated by her assessment despite the many remaining hours before the mirror would be ready for its final figuring stage. "Worth every hour," he said. "Store-bought telescopes never feel quite like this."
