Steam rose from the compost windrows as Fitzwilliam checked the internal temperature with a long probe thermometer, confirming the pile had reached the proper heat needed to break down this week's collected food scraps. Urban composting demanded careful monitoring most people never considered.

His facility partner, Marisol, operated the turning machine, methodically working through the row to aerate the decomposing material and maintain the oxygen levels essential for proper composting. "This row's cooking nicely," she called over the machine's engine noise.

Fitzwilliam confirmed the temperature reading matched what he'd expect from a properly heating pile, satisfied with this batch's progress toward becoming finished compost for the city's community gardens. "One hundred forty degrees," he said. "Right where we want it."

Marisol finished turning the row and moved to the next one waiting for its scheduled aeration, continuing the facility's constant rotation through dozens of active compost piles at various stages. "Row twelve's ready for finishing," she called out.

Fitzwilliam checked row twelve's texture and smell, confirming it had transformed from raw scraps into the dark, earthy finished compost that would eventually enrich soil across the city's growing network of gardens. "Beautiful compost," he said. "This is why we do this work."
