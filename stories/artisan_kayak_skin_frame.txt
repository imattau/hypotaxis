Driftwood ribs curved gracefully as Nanuq lashed another joint of the traditional skin-on-frame kayak, using techniques his grandfather had taught him for building vessels capable of handling arctic waters. Traditional kayak building demanded exact proportions.

His apprentice, Sila, prepared the sealskin covering, scraping it to the precise thickness that would provide both waterproofing and the flexibility needed to stretch properly over the completed wooden frame. "Skin's scraped and ready," she said.

Nanuq examined the frame's developing shape, checking the symmetry against traditional proportions passed down through generations before continuing the demanding lashing work. "This frame's coming together beautifully," he said.

Sila watched him continue lashing joints with sinew cord, absorbing the traditional knot techniques that created strong, flexible connections without any metal fasteners throughout the entire frame structure. "How many more joints remain?" she asked.

Nanuq counted the remaining unlashed connections, calculating the work still needed before this particular kayak would be ready for its sealskin covering and eventual maiden voyage. "About twenty more," he said. "Then she's ready for the sea."
