The rain had not stopped for three days, and the river below the bridge had risen almost to the old stone railings. Kessa stood at the edge of the market square, watching the water carry broken crates and torn awnings downstream. She had lived in this city her whole life, but she had never seen the river climb so high.

Her uncle Bram ran the grain stall two streets over, and it was his voice she heard now, shouting over the noise of the crowd. He was trying to move the last of his sacks to higher ground before the water reached the square. Kessa pushed through the press of bodies to reach him. "Uncle, the west bridge is already underwater," she said, breathless. Bram did not look up from the sack he was tying. "Then we go around," he said. "Help me with this."

Together they hauled the sacks onto a cart, working in silence as thunder rolled somewhere beyond the hills. The market was emptying fast now, merchants abandoning stalls they could not save. A boy ran past shouting that the eastern dam had cracked. Kessa felt her stomach tighten. Her mother was still at the mill, on the wrong side of the river.

"Go," Bram said, seeing her face. "I can manage the cart alone. Go find your mother." Kessa did not wait to be told twice. She left the cart and ran, her boots slipping on the wet cobblestones, weaving between carts and shouting figures until the market square gave way to the narrower streets that led toward the mill.

By the time she reached the mill road, the water was already ankle-deep, cold and fast-moving, dragging at her legs with every step. The mill itself stood dark against the grey sky, its great wheel motionless, jammed by debris that had piled up against the sluice gate. Kessa called out for her mother, but her voice was swallowed by the roar of the water pouring over the broken gate.

Inside, she found the mill floor already flooding, sacks of flour split open and dissolving into pale sludge. Her mother, Ilsa, was on the upper platform, struggling to free a length of rope tangled around the main support beam. "Kessa!" Ilsa called down. "The ladder's gone, the water took it!" Kessa looked around desperately for anything she could use to reach the platform.

She found an old grain chute leaning against the far wall and dragged it through the rising water, setting it against the platform's edge as a makeshift ramp. It groaned under her weight as she climbed, but it held. Ilsa reached down and pulled her the last few feet onto the platform, both of them soaked and shaking.

"We can't stay here," Kessa said, looking at the water still climbing the support beams below. "The whole structure could go." Ilsa nodded grimly and pointed toward a narrow window at the platform's far end, one that opened onto the millrace embankment. It was a difficult climb, but it was dry ground beyond.

They moved carefully along the platform, the old wood creaking ominously beneath them. Halfway across, a support beam gave way with a sudden crack, and the platform tilted sharply. Ilsa grabbed a support post just in time, but Kessa lost her footing and slid toward the edge, catching herself only by grabbing a length of chain bolted to the wall.

"Kessa!" Ilsa shouted, scrambling toward her. Kessa's fingers were slipping on the wet chain, the water churning directly below her now, thick with debris. "Don't let go," Ilsa said, reaching her at last and hauling her back onto the more stable section of the platform. For a moment they simply held onto each other, breathing hard, as the mill groaned around them.

They reached the narrow window at last and climbed through onto the embankment, the rain still falling but the ground here solid beneath their feet. Looking back, they watched the mill's great wheel finally tear loose from its housing and vanish into the flood, carried away like everything else the river had claimed that day.

By evening the rain had eased to a drizzle, and the water in the market square had begun, slowly, to recede. Bram found them on the embankment road, the cart of grain sacks safe behind him, and for the first time that day Kessa let herself cry, more from relief than from fear. Ilsa held her daughter close and watched the grey water carry the last of the wreckage toward the sea.
