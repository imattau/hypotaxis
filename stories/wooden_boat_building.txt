Cedar shavings piled beneath Bartholomew's plane as he shaped another strip for the small boat's hull, working the wood to match the curve his mentor had taught him decades ago in this exact workshop. The strip-built canoe was nearly ready for its final sanding.

His son, Cornelius, home for the summer before starting his own boatbuilding apprenticeship elsewhere, fitted the shaped strip against the hull's growing form, checking the gap before gluing it into position. "This one fits almost perfectly," he said, testing the joint.

Bartholomew examined the fit himself, running a finger along the seam to check for any gaps that might compromise the hull's eventual watertight integrity. "Good eye," he said. "A little glue and clamp pressure, and that seam disappears completely."

Cornelius applied the glue carefully along the strip's edge and pressed it into position, securing it with clamps while Bartholomew prepared the next strip for shaping. "How many more strips until the hull's complete?" he asked.

Bartholomew counted the remaining gaps in the hull's developing shape, calculating the work still ahead before this canoe would be ready for water. "About fifteen more," he said. "Then we finally get to see if she floats true."
