Soot particles drifted through the workshop's light as Guillermina ground the carbon pigment against her stone slab, mixing it gradually with the binding agent that would eventually become traditional calligraphy ink. This exact process hadn't changed in centuries.

Her apprentice, Ferdinand, prepared the next batch of pine soot, sifting it through fine mesh to remove any coarse particles that would create unwanted texture in the finished ink. "This batch looks finer than usual," he said.

Guillermina examined the sifted soot he offered, testing a small pinch between her fingers to confirm its consistency matched the standard required for high-quality ink production. "Excellent quality," she said. "Let's incorporate this into the current batch."

Ferdinand added the fine soot gradually as Guillermina continued grinding, watching the mixture darken and thicken toward the glossy black consistency that indicated proper ink formation. "The color's getting really deep now," he observed.

Guillermina tested the ink's flow with a calligraphy brush, satisfied with how smoothly it moved across the test paper before beginning the process of forming this batch into traditional ink sticks. "Perfect batch," she said. "This will make beautiful calligraphy."
