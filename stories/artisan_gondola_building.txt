Asymmetrical hull curved gracefully beneath Marco's careful plane strokes, the traditional gondola's distinctive shape emerging from carefully selected timber according to techniques passed down through generations of Venetian boatbuilders. Authentic gondolas required specific proportions.

His apprentice, Giulietta, prepared the metal fittings that would eventually adorn the finished hull, polishing the traditional ferro decoration that identified this particular workshop's distinctive maritime craftsmanship. "The ferro is polished and ready," she said.

Marco examined the asymmetrical curve he'd been shaping, checking it against centuries-old proportional guidelines that gave gondolas their famous ability to be rowed by a single oarsman standing at the stern. "This asymmetry looks correct," he said.

Giulietta watched him continue shaping the hull, absorbing the subtle technique that balanced the boat's left and right sides differently to compensate for the single-oar rowing style unique to Venice's traditional watercraft. "Why exactly is it asymmetrical?" she asked.

Marco continued his careful shaping, explaining the physics that generations of boatbuilders had intuited long before anyone formally understood the engineering principles involved. "Compensates for the single oar," he said. "Otherwise she'd just go in circles."
